title: Tomato late blight
---
## Symptoms
Large dark, water-soaked (wet-looking) black or brown patches on leaves and stems that spread quickly until the whole leaf dies. In damp weather a white fuzzy growth appears on the patches, often under the leaf. Fruit gets firm, dark brown, greasy-looking patches and then rots.

## Why it spreads
Caused by the water mould Phytophthora infestans. Spores are carried by wind and can travel long distances. It spreads fastest with cool nights, warm days and long periods of wet leaves from rain or fog (spore production is best around 18-22 °C). It can destroy a whole field within days.

## What to do now
Act within 24 hours. Pull out badly infected plants, put them in a bag or bury them deep, and do not leave them in a pile in the field. Do not compost them. Protect the healthy plants immediately with a fungicide spray and repeat as the label says. Tell neighbouring farmers, because the spores spread on the wind.

## Organic and low-cost options
Copper-based fungicides are the main option allowed in organic farming; they protect healthy leaves but cannot cure infected ones, so spray before or at the very first sign. Good drainage and removing infected plants quickly are essential.

## Chemical options
TNAU lists sprays such as mancozeb, zineb, cyazofamid, azoxystrobin, or azoxystrobin + difenoconazole, at the rates on the product label (for example cyazofamid 34.5% SC at 80 ml per acre). Protectant fungicides must be applied before infection to work well. Alternate products with different modes of action and follow the label and pre-harvest interval.

## Prevention next season
Use healthy seedlings. Ensure good drainage, rotate crops, and remove volunteer tomato and potato plants and culled potatoes, which keep the disease alive. TNAU recommends applying Trichoderma asperellum to the soil (about 1 kg per acre). Watch the crop closely in cool, wet weather.
